There is a shift in perspective here — from viewing technology as standalone objects with fixed purposes to viewing technology as things that can be endlessly combined into new configurations.

This shift is not merely abstract; it actually reflects a major transformation currently underway in the role of technology itself. The old industrial process technologies that defined the manufacturing economy — like open-hearth steelmaking or the cracking process for refining crude oil — were, to a large extent, fixed. They produced one thing in one place, processed a specific raw material into a specific industrial output, and mostly operated in separate, independent factories. But now these relatively self-contained process technologies are giving way to a different form of technology. These new technologies can be easily combined, forming technology modules that can be used over and over again. GPS technology can directly provide positioning, but it cannot function in isolation. It is used as an element, combined with other elements to navigate planes and ships, assist land surveying, and plan agricultural operations. It is like a highly reactive chemical component (e.g., a hydroxide ion) — participating only a little on its own, yet dominating various combinations. The same applies to other elements of the digital revolution: algorithms, switches, routers, relays, web services, and so on. Likewise, we can describe the elements that make up modern genetic engineering or nanotechnology in the same way — they can be matched together in limitless combinations, assembled and reassembled for different purposes, and they can enable the further combination of building-block-like integrated modules.

Modern technology is not merely a collection of somewhat independent means of production; it has evolved into an open language for creating economic structures and functions. Slowly, we are moving from technologies that produce fixed physical products to technologies that can be endlessly combined and assembled for new purposes.

Technology, once a means of production, is evolving into a kind of "chemistry."

How should we understand these two perspectives? How should we view technology from each angle? How do we explain the evolution of technology?

# Thoughts

From the **external view of technology**, technology is understood as a whole — its principles and specific implementation details are not considered. We focus only on how it manifests and how it interacts with the outside world. Take short-form video as an example: when we discuss short-form video, whether it's about degrading human attention spans or shifting how we use fragmented time, the discussion stays at the level of our interaction with it. Even when we talk about its recommendation mechanisms (filter bubbles), the concern is limited to the video content being recommended.

From the **internal view of technology**, the principles of technology and how it develops should be brought into consideration. In fact, we should consider the process of technological invention and application. The most fundamental question is: what is technology, and how does it evolve? The earliest technologies arose from principles and observation. New technologies are assembled from old ones, and new technologies in turn provide the foundation for future technologies.
