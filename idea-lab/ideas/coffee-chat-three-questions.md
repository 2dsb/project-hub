# Coffee Chat: The Three Questions — Work, Best, Talent

**One sentence**: In a coffee chat, three questions in ascending order of intimacy — introduce your work, show what you do best, name your strongest talent — carry the conversation from public information to the generative source of the person's ability, with the third aimed at *absorption* rather than mere information.

## The Three Questions

```
1. Work    — "Tell me about your work."          → what they do
2. Best    — "What are you best at?"             → where their edge is
3. Talent  — "What's your strongest talent?"     → what comes naturally to them
```

## The Escalation Ladder

The three questions are not a list of independent prompts — they are ordered, and the order does the work:

| # | Question | What it reaches | Why it's answerable |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Introduce your work | The **public** layer — role, project, domain | Easiest: work is what they talk about all day |
| 2 | What you're best at | The **peak** layer — where they exceed peers | Flattering, requires self-assessment, still professional |
| 3 | Your strongest talent | The **innate** layer — the source that produces the peak | Rarely asked, so the answer is unpolished and personal |

Each step earns the next. Q1 is safe and supplies the raw material; Q2 asks them to rank themselves and reveals the edge; Q3 asks them to name the thing *behind* the edge. You cannot open with Q3 — it reads as an interrogation when it hasn't been paid for by the two softer questions.

## Why Q3 Is the Distinctive Move

The third question is not information gathering; it is **an attempt at absorption**. Q1 and Q2 describe *what someone has*; Q3 tries to locate *what generates it* — the underlying capability that produced both the work and the edge.

That reframes the whole conversation's purpose. A normal coffee chat trades information. This one treats the other person as a source of a transferable capability, and asks the one question most likely to expose the mechanism.

Two secondary effects make it unusually strong:

- **It's rarely asked.** People are asked about their work constantly (Q1) and about their strengths occasionally (Q2). Almost nobody asks them to name their talent. The question is memorable for exactly that reason, and it's hard to answer on autopilot — you get a real answer rather than a rehearsed one.
- **It's a high compliment, structurally.** Treating someone as having a *talent* — a distinctive capacity rather than a job — is a stronger form of respect than praising their work. (Compare [[attribution-of-achievement]], which flatters via causal interest.)

## The Qualification: Absorption Has a Ceiling

Talent is largely tacit, and asking about it does not transfer it. Three limits:

1. **They may not know.** Talents are frequently invisible from the inside — the person may have never had to articulate it, and may name a *label* ("I'm good with people") rather than a mechanism.
2. **Label ≠ mechanism.** "I'm good with people" is not installable. What you want is the *how* — which usually requires follow-up questions chasing the answer, exactly as [[attribution-of-achievement]] chases each attribution vertically.
3. **Talent may be non-transferable in principle.** Some of it is temperament or accumulated history rather than a technique. What actually transfers is more often the **pointer** — what to practice, what to attend to — than the capacity itself.

So the honest framing is: Q3 has a high ceiling on *insight* and a lower ceiling on *transfer*. It reliably tells you where to look; it does not reliably hand you the ability.

## Delivered Badly

The question carries a risk: asked flatly, "what's your strongest talent" can read as instrumental — sizing someone up as a resource. The failure is in the framing rather than the question. It works when genuine admiration is already established (which is why it sits third, after two rounds of demonstrated interest) and fails when it's the opening move.
