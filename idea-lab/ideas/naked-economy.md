# Chapter 1

Individuals want a happier life, and businesses want maximum returns on their investments, but both have limited resources and must make tradeoffs.

In the market, prices connect supply and demand, reflect scarcity, and serve as signals that create incentives — prompting people to reassess tradeoffs and adjust their behavior.

When the price of milk tea rises, consumers re-weigh "the pleasure of milk tea" against "the money spent" and buy less of it. If the higher price makes it more profitable, businesses become more willing to purchase ingredients and produce more milk tea. A rising milk tea price reflects an increase in demand relative to supply, and by altering incentives, it guides both individuals and businesses to re-evaluate their tradeoffs.

# Chapter 2

Chapter 2 explains that for markets to function effectively, they need more than just prices — they also require a foundation of rules and corrective mechanisms. Governments reduce transaction costs by providing property rights, contracts, law, currency, and infrastructure. When externalities cause prices to miss social costs or benefits, governments correct incentives through taxes, restrictions, or subsidies, bringing those external effects into the decision-maker's tradeoff calculus.

"Internalizing externalities" can be understood in plain terms:

When someone's actions affect others, make that effect show up on their own decision ledger as much as possible.

**Negative externalities** are about "adding the missing costs back in."

Take factory pollution as an example:

Originally, when a business produces goods, it only accounts for raw materials, labor, and equipment costs.
But the pollution — worsening air quality, residents falling ill, environmental remediation costs — is borne by others.
The business's ledger has none of these costs, so it sees production as cheap and produces too much.
A pollution tax imposed by the government adds these social costs back onto the business's ledger.
Once the business sees higher costs, it will reduce polluting production or switch to cleaner technology.

**Positive externalities** are about "making up for the missing benefits."

Take vaccines as an example:

A person who gets vaccinated not only protects themselves but also reduces the probability of infecting others.
Yet when making the decision, they may only calculate their own benefits and costs, leaving out the benefit of "protecting others."
As a result, too few people in society get vaccinated.
When the government provides free vaccines or subsidizes vaccination, it converts a portion of the social benefit into personal incentive.
With the personal cost of vaccination lowered, people are more willing to get vaccinated.

So the point is not simply "the government restores prices to their optimum," but rather:

The government tries to ensure that individuals and businesses face a more complete ledger of costs and benefits.

# Chapter 3

The central thread of Chapter 3 is incentives, because institutions change how people choose.

With public resources, the incentive an individual faces is this: take a little more of the shared resource, keep the full benefit for yourself, while the depletion caused by your consumption is spread across everyone. So each person takes a little extra, leading to the depletion of the shared resource.

With taxation, the incentive individuals or businesses face is that paying taxes costs money, so they will look for leaks — shifting assets, evading, and so on.

With principal-agent relationships, the agent's incentives may revolve around bonuses and job security, which do not align with shareholder interests. The agent may maximize their own utility without regard for shareholder interests.

Therefore, the key to institutional design is not just looking at the policy's stated intent, but seeing the institution as a set of incentives — and watching how individuals and businesses re-weigh their choices in response, and what outcomes actually result.

In exam involution, the payoff comes mainly from relative position (ranking), while the cost is actual time spent, stress, and opportunities forgone — exercise, social life, rest. When one student studies more, they may improve their rank, so it is rational for them individually. But when everyone studies more, the overall ranking structure does not improve, yet everyone bears higher costs. So the mechanism by which individual rationality leads to a collectively worse outcome is this: relative gains cannot be increased for everyone simultaneously, but absolute costs are genuinely borne by everyone.

Private benefit + shared cost: public resources, pollution.
Relative benefit + absolute cost: involution, arms races, positional competition.

This is an incentive problem, because each person responding to personal incentives under the current rules is acting rationally — but when everyone does so, the collective outcome can worsen. Institutional design must therefore make it so that the choices that feel worthwhile to individuals also tend to produce better collective outcomes.

**Institutional design changes the costs and benefits individuals face by rewriting the rules, prompting them to re-weigh their choices, so that individually rational decisions lead as much as possible to better collective outcomes.**

# Chapter 4

Regulation exists to address market externalities, the abuse of public resources, and dangerous transactions — but it is subject to political processes, which can produce "regulatory costs" such as corruption, resource misallocation, and stifled innovation. The key, then, is to use well-designed incentives so that individual choices align as closely as possible with the overall interest of society.

Chapter 4 serves as a correction to Chapter 2. Chapter 2 emphasizes that governments lower transaction costs by providing institutional foundations — property rights, law, currency, infrastructure — and correct market failures like externalities through regulation, taxation, or subsidies. But Chapter 4 reminds us that real-world governments also fail: government agencies lack competitive pressure and may be inefficient; governments have limited information and may misallocate resources; political processes may channel resources toward the powerful or entrenched interest groups; taxation alters behavior around work, investment, and consumption, creating deadweight loss; and poorly designed enforcement can create new perverse incentives.

Therefore, public goods do not have to be produced directly by the government. They can be financed and planned by the government, then executed by the private sector through competitive bidding — combining the government's ability to solve the supply-shortage problem with the market's ability to drive efficiency through competition.

Distinguish between "what ought to be" and "what actually is."
