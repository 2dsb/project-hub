In notes 6, 7, and 8, others and society influence the self in two ways:

1. **Common sense**: They promote value tendencies, and by influencing (if I may say so) my experiences, they fill my mind with biased common sense. This common sense shapes the way we think and how we perceive the world — but it is all imposed by society.
2. **Behavior**: When everyone else gravitates toward doing one thing, you come to believe that thing is worth pursuing. If you don't follow suit — if you pursue your own way of living, for instance — you will face doubt, misunderstanding, and discouragement.

Explaining some phenomena from this angle:

- **Infantilization**: Helicopter parents supervise their children from a young age. Children grow up under parental management, which is equivalent to parents imposing experiences on them that create the common-sense belief that "children should constantly report their progress to their parents."
- **Loss of the ability to feel and care for oneself**: The family environment forces children to suppress their own needs, leading to a "lack of values" (is this a subset of common-sense influence, or parallel to it?)
- **Self-confidence built on success**: Parents impose perfectionism on their children, who are forced to meet parental expectations. But this phenomenon is internalized as common sense — they become perfectionistic toward themselves, base their self-confidence on success, and feel threatened by others' success.

# The Negative Effects of Common Sense

Possessing so-called common sense inherently implies a lack of the capacity for thinking. In university, this leads to not knowing what to do, having no direction, and consequently being more susceptible to herd mentality.

# The Value of Thinking: Resisting the Invasion of Common Sense

From elementary school onward, your mind has been filled with common sense. This common sense not only influences your behavior but also shapes your way of thinking. In our cognition, common sense is self-evident and unquestionably correct.

Until one day, a person or a book reveals the existence of this common sense to you. You learn new ways of thinking from professors and mentors. Through extensive reading, and through moving from building consensus with peers to overturning consensus, you engage in active thinking — first recognizing common sense, then questioning it, and finally examining it from new angles. After this series of actions, the socially indoctrinated "common sense" in your mind is fully transformed into wisdom born of your own thinking.

This common sense can be divided into two dimensions: one relates to the public; the other relates to the self.

**Common sense related to the public:**

For example, the value orientation in society and universities can lead you to form the common-sense belief that "the higher you are in the social pyramid, the better." If you accept this without thinking, you may end up as a high-ranking person who is indifferent to the status quo and incapable of feeling discontent.

Volunteering is promoted in society as "adding sparkle to your resume" and "demonstrating personal character." This can cause you to instinctively (without your own thinking) set the server and the served in opposition — not humble, but rather smug about your own moral superiority.

Why does society tend to favor DIY entrepreneurs rather than making structural changes at the economic or political level? This is because there is a popular notion in society that "being a politician requires starting from grassroots positions — not worth it — whereas a DIY entrepreneur can also make society better." Between the two paths for changing society, they choose the easier one. But because they don't think, they fail to realize that the former is the fundamental change. As for people at elite schools, they thrive in the current system (but don't think), so they believe the existing framework is fine and needs no major improvement. They only know how to make small tweaks under the existing system — like DIY ventures.

**Common sense related to the self:**

Some of it concerns one's own identity. For example: Some children regard themselves as appendages of their parents. As a result, in university they pursue a double degree, with one major chosen to satisfy their parents' wishes. This also causes their ultimate goals to be shaped by their parents' unfulfilled ideals.

# How to Learn to Think

By actively engaging with ideas and perspectives, and using your innate capacity for thinking to analyze them and integrate them with past thoughts.

"The teacher can give you new perspectives; peers build consensus and then overturn it" — in practice, this means actively gathering ideas from different individuals and deciding between them through debate, as the saying goes: "Listen to both sides and you will be enlightened."

# The Value of Thinking

First, by becoming aware of common sense itself, you will live more alertly. When making decisions, you can analyze problems from the perspective of common sense and make the decision that is "most in your own interest" (interest here in the broad sense).

Through thinking at the level of the self, you can build the self:
- Build a bridge between ideals and reality
- Build a bridge between emotion and thought

After gaining a deeper understanding of yourself, you will come to know what you are passionate about (career), and use that as your lifelong direction.

# The Influence of Others' Behavior on Individual Thought

The salmon race — when everyone is doing one thing, you end up doing that thing too.

For example, all the students from the previous cohort who did competitions got very high scores on the college entrance exam, and Teacher Dong expected me to score 590 as well. So internally, I felt a sense of guilt for "not studying the regular curriculum."
