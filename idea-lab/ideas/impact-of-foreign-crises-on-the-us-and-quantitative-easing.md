Similar to the US, Country A also experienced a "Great Depression" crisis, which led to:

- Investors pulled out capital, and local banks faced bankruptcy.
- The IMF was created as a result, borrowing US dollars from the US and delivering them to Country A's government.
- However, people in Country A still produced little but consumed a lot, making repayment impossible.
- At the same time, such news diverted attention and eased the US crisis.
- Naturally, US prices and consumption both declined.

The US government implemented a new plan: quantitative easing.
- It injected capital into the market by investing in projects, rather than directly handing money to the public.
- For example, investing in distressed enterprises and offering low-interest loans to students.
- As a result, people began consuming again, and prices started to rise.

But as soon as quantitative easing stopped, the economic bubble began to burst.
- Therefore, the government pursued a plan of "indefinite quantitative easing."
- This resulted in inflation and persistently high unemployment.
- It led to the Occupy Wall Street movement.

In the next election:
- In the presidential race, Okuda harnessed public anger over class inequality, advocated for economic revival by raising taxes on the rich, and ultimately defeated his opponent Rufei, who came from Wall Street.
- They claimed to the public that if banks did not issue more US dollars, the next thing to face a full-scale collapse would be imminent.
- Meanwhile, to avoid the difficult decision of cutting spending, the Senate implemented a scheme called "Ocean Explorer," addressing the "fiscal cliff" through modest tax increases and so-called "spending cuts."
