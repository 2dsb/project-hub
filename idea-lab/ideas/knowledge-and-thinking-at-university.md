# Knowledge and Thinking

In *Excellent Sheep*,

Chapter 7 discusses the value of thinking: washing away common sense, re-examining what I believe to be correct but haven't fully thought through.
"Recognize common sense, question it, and rethink it from new angles."

Chapter 9 discusses the "process of knowledge production."
- Natural sciences: understanding domain assumptions, then conducting further analysis
- Humanities: dialectical engagement between old and new knowledge

## The Relationship Between the Two

- What exactly does "the process of knowledge production" refer to?
    - For natural sciences, it is "understanding domain assumptions, then conducting further analysis"; for humanities, it is "dialectical engagement between old and new knowledge."
    - At university, I should test the validity of this claim:
    - If I treat my past understanding of university subjects as a blank slate, this book has now instilled in me the following "common sense": for natural sciences, knowledge production involves 1. understanding domain assumptions and structure, 2. conducting further analysis based on that structure; for humanities, knowledge production lies in "dialectical engagement between old and new knowledge."
    - How to verify the validity and limitations of this "common sense"?
    - I can first assume the common sense is correct, i.e.:
    - For STEM fields, such as mathematics:
        - On one hand, we should try to find evidence that **proves** its knowledge originates from further analysis of domain assumptions — this raises several questions, such as:
        - In the discipline of "mathematics," what is "knowledge"?
        - How should its domain framework be characterized? (i.e., what is the organizational structure of "knowledge" within the discipline of mathematics?)
        - If someone understands the answers to the above two questions, they can understand:
            - Starting from zero, the path to learning mathematics
        - On the other hand, we can try to find evidence that **refutes** the claim that its knowledge originates from further analysis of domain assumptions:
        - This also raises several questions, such as:
        - Does mathematics necessarily contain "domain assumptions"?
      - For humanities, I can
        - On one hand, try to find evidence that **proves** its knowledge originates from "dialectical engagement between old and new knowledge":
        - Several questions:
        - Within a specific discipline, what is "knowledge"?
        - What is the organizational structure of knowledge?
        - On the other hand, we can try to find evidence that **refutes** the claim:
        - Could the production of humanities knowledge have sources beyond "dialectical engagement between old and new knowledge"?
    - Does this mean we can distinguish humanities from sciences based on the nature and compositional structure of knowledge?
- How does understanding the process of knowledge production assist thinking?
    - From the perspective of a specific discipline, you can analyze common sense within that domain (all knowledge, practice, and experience) — analogous to **standard engineering** in technical terms.
    - "Thinking" can be understood as solving a particular problem. One level of abstraction up: once you learn different ways of combining knowledge, you can use that to anticipate the steps of a solution (= creating a technique whose goal is solving that problem).
- Beyond this, what other methods can help thinking?
     - If we treat "thinking" as the innovation of a singular technique, what we need to do is:
        - Understand and become familiar with as many domains as possible and accumulate experience within them.
      - What kind of teachers should we seek out?
        - Erudite teachers know more domains and have more experience within those domains.
        - In particular, if humanities teachers discuss their own lives in class, it means they not only know about the existence of certain domains but also have experience with the grammar of those domains.
      - What is the student's role?
        - For an individual, their thinking pattern (definition: treating thinking as problem-solving, the domains they habitually use and the grammar within those domains remains similar) does not vary much across different problems.
        - But thinking patterns vary greatly between individuals.
        - The process of establishing and overturning common sense with fellow students can be understood as an exchange of domains and methods of using knowledge within those domains.
            - This means, through this process, I can learn about others' "habitual domains" and their habitual modes of expression within those domains.
        - This allows me to become familiar with more domains and gain experience using them.
