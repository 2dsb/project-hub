The load-bearing principle: **symmetric exchange, not extraction.** Approach every person as a two-way exchange — each side holds first-hand truth the other lacks. Map the exchange before the first message: what you have that they need, what they have that you need. If no genuine give-item exists, the exchange doesn't hold. Never reduce the person to a free consultant, and never jump from a first meeting to asking for favors.

**Before the interaction.** *Position the node* — fix in one line what this person is for (data point, bridge source, dual-value node, or deep-dig candidate), and re-check it mid-meeting when you catch yourself warming to a direction. *Classify the edge* — Connection / Information / Channel / Endorsement, plus bonding-vs-bridging and broker-or-not; an Information edge carries no endorsement weight, so their referral is a warm handoff, not a backer's name. *Separate signal from exchange item* — a credibility signal (competition or math background) opens the door and decays fast; an exchange item is specific knowledge you actually possess and trade, built over 3–6 months, not learned (faking it always gets exposed). *Pre-research and pre-design* — this is what decides whether a domain-mapping first meeting becomes an exchange or a lecture. Learn their working vocabulary (tools, papers, frameworks) so you can answer one sentence when they go specific: this lowers their explanation cost and is what makes them treat you as a near-peer instead of teaching from zero. Pre-design a fixed grid of what you're collecting (subdivisions / who's playing / the entry path / your math gap / the crossover to your main line), so you leave with a complete card rather than scattered impressions. Prepare two or three hooks, each collecting a different asset (subdivision / judgment / bridge) — a good hook surfaces what web research can't reach. Design both sides in advance: your give (the framework-plus-question and the two or three facts you'll deliver) and your take (the checklist you'll ask them to mark right or wrong). Know ahead of time what you must not assert — the domain facts you can't source, which the target knows better than you. Pre-research buys the right to ask well; it never substitutes for understanding, and it's a finite resource to spend on your top one or two cells rather than spread thin. Enter through a warm intro and control how the introducer frames you. *Cap the active set* — hold only a few simultaneous relationships, open a new person only when a slot frees.

**During the interaction.** *Open with an answerable compliment, then a motive question* — praise a concrete decision they made, then convert it into a question they can expand on. *Listen first, then climb* — ask about their motives before revealing yours, progress fact → experience → advice, and close with a completeness question ("what should I ask that I haven't thought of?"). Match the probing mode to their track record: attribution (chasing "why" vertically, "are these all" horizontally) for high-achievers, plain advice-seeking for ordinary juniors. *Give at least half of what you take* — run live self-checks (has one-way asking passed halfway, am I warming to a direction that's only a data point, am I explaining myself a second time). Give as framework-plus-question, never assertion; show your signal only through question quality, never self-declaration. *Trigger the exchange on their identity, not their emotion* — a stranger won't voice their anxiety, so enter from your own uncertainty or ask directly. *Bridge in aggregate, then name once* — ask "how many in your class do X, where do they end up" before any names. *Ask for a pointer, never an introduction* — an introduction stakes the referrer's reputation (a Spence cost a fresh tie can't support), while a pointer is cheap information transfer; say "you don't need to introduce me," and cap pointer requests at a small number per period.

**After the interaction.** *Log a first-class note* — the one thing only this person's perspective could supply — plus a give/take ledger. *Follow up within 24 hours asking nothing*, and *report back within 30 seconds* when you act on their advice or pointer (feedback is pure repayment, never a new ask); repay the referrer too, since they staked their reputation. *Triangulate before trusting* — one person is one data point, so cross-check every judgment and pointer against three independent sources and verify an introduced person's identity in the field yourself. *Run event-driven, compounding rounds* — a few per year, triggered by real milestones, each bringing and taking one new asset with content never repeating — and audit monthly, downgrading anyone who yields nothing for two straight months.

**Cross-cutting.** Information is cheap, endorsement is expensive — earn introductions with a track record, not elapsed time. Most people are bridging weak ties, not strong ties, so don't upgrade a pleasant talk into a deep relationship. Time actions to events, not the calendar. And hold the red lines: no one-way extraction, no manufactured signals, no escalating relationship strength, no using ascribed or family channels to reach someone.

---

**Footnote.** Pre-research is not only preparation for the meeting — it is itself a way of understanding the field. The briefing you assemble before a meeting is already a product of the information-collection line (线二); it yields durable domain understanding whether or not the meeting itself pays off. Its method (tentative) is the layered field map — math / tech / business — applied to the target's domain.
