A discipline's boundaries are subjective convention, not essential truth — there is no intrinsic answer to whether two disciplines "should" be separate. I propose a three-factor model for how disciplines get divided:

- **Intrinsic complexity** — static and stable; theorems don't get simpler with time.
- **Mechanicalness (current level of understanding)** — cognitive and time-varying; it can flip. Stereo matching, for instance, went from hand-written algorithms to neural networks.
- **Position in the knowledge graph** — relational; it shifts as the graph grows.

The three factors interlock: when a discipline's position changes, its mechanicalness follows. Knowledge gets absorbed into a larger learning system (neural rendering absorbing graphics), so the discipline moves from "independent discipline" to "embedded component," and its mechanicalness flips from "understood as a whole" to "a small piece inside a non-mechanical system." This is the mechanism of attachment — a mechanical knowledge architecture attaches onto a non-mechanical learning architecture rather than standing alone as its own discipline.

A discipline's internal structure, meanwhile, is defined by a small set of core materials it revolves around — graphics and vision both orbit only three things: 2D images, 3D scenes, and the camera. So the boundary between them degrades from "essential" to "conventional."
