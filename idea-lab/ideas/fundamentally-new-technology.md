Standard engineering understands new technology as iterative updates on existing technology, forming a "Darwinian" process.

The question is: are there other modes of technological innovation — for example, new technology not based on existing technology?

# Standard Technology vs. Fundamentally New Technology

The defining characteristic of standard engineering is that, when facing a requirement (as a goal), the technical principles it attempts to construct are relatively clear.

Consider an experienced engineer A, tasked with building a faster aircraft. A can "model" the design on past technology (slower aircraft of other types) to determine which sub-technologies to use and how they connect. In short, at the principle level, past practice and experience are sufficient — the engineer does not need to "invent" new principles. From a problem-solving perspective, the large problem's solution is distributed across several sub-problems concerning sub-technologies, but the engineer knows that if those sub-problems are solved, the large problem will also be solved.

From this we see: **in standard engineering, the principle of the new technology is identical to the principle of some past technology; the only thing that changes is the requirements placed on the sub-technologies that compose it.**

- At the phenomenon level: identical principles mean the "primary phenomena" employed by the technology, and the way phenomena are combined, are exactly the same as in past technology.
- At the problem-solving level: the solution path for the large problem is already known; only the secondary sub-problems need solving.
- At the linguistic expression level: both the new and old technology use the same "words" (their primary components) and "syntax" (their mode of combination).

Now let us consider its opposite: **fundamentally new technology**.

**Fundamentally new technology: a new technology that, for an existing purpose, adopts a new principle to achieve its goal** (a new principle meaning one different from that of existing technology).

The difference between the two lies in **whether a new principle has been created**.

# How Is Fundamentally New Technology Generated?

Like standard engineering, it arises from some requirement and divides into a mental side and a physical side.

The mental side includes: conceiving a principle (a conceptual solution) and configuring the corresponding sub-technologies for it.

## Finding the Principle

From one perspective, a technology corresponds to a principle, and a principle tells us "how phenomena are employed."

**In essence, we only need to construct a "solution" (a chain) with one end connected to the requirement and the other end connected to phenomena.**

The two major modes of invention are thus:
- Starting from a given purpose or requirement, discovering a principle that can realize it.
- Starting from some phenomenon or effect, gradually embedding a principle for how to use it.

This process can be compared to climbing a snowy mountain:
- Standard engineering: the overall route is known; only the details of each segment need to be handled.
- Fundamentally new technology: the overall route is unknown.
- A conceived solution = a conceived rough path (details undetermined).

General steps (assuming we start from a requirement):
1. The requirement is transformed into a thoroughly described problem.
2. Conceive some solutions:
   - A solution is not concrete, but it specifies which phenomena should be used, the method for combining phenomena, which domain to draw from, etc.
   - There can be multiple such solutions.

### Where Do Solutions Come From?
- Sometimes, principles are "borrowed": they originally served a different purpose, even from a different domain.
- Sometimes, principles come from combining prior concepts.
- Sometimes, they come from reviewing the past, from theory, or from discussions with colleagues.

**In all cases, principles come from existing devices, methods, theories, or functions, bearing the character of "appropriation."**

### Inspiration: The Process of Discovering Principles at the Mental Level

Solutions sometimes emerge suddenly — this is called inspiration:

For the discoverer, such a solution appears so complete, as if it had already been assembled in the subconscious. And so, when inspiration strikes, people simply "know it is right" and accept its validity.

### Qualities Required for Conceiving Principles

1. Mental association: familiarity with various domains (function libraries).
2. Imagination: the ability to envision the solution.

**Recursiveness**: technology is recursive (technology is composed of sub-technologies), and clearly this design method can also be understood as recursive.
- Once the principle has been fully conceived, the next step is "clarification" — specifying the concrete mode of employing phenomena (understood as conceiving the purposes, principles, and subordinate sub-technologies of the sub-technologies contained within the "principle").
- Using the snowy mountain analogy: after the rough route is determined, the specific route for each segment is then figured out.
- If "we have already traversed this segment before" — that is, it is contained within the principle of some existing technology (meaning we know how to implement it) — its solution may be adopted directly.

## The Materialization of Concepts (Physical Side)

Realizing a conceived principle at the physical level, turning it into a technology, is called "materialization of concepts."

# Generalization

In fact, this is not unique to technological innovation; similar paradigms also appear in science and mathematics:

Technology: 1. Conceive a principle. 2. Realize the principle in the real world, turning it into a technology.

Science: 1. Conceive a rough conceptual approach. 2. Systematize the approach, turning it into a complete theory.

Mathematics: 1. The mathematician intuits one or two primary principles. 2. Supplement secondary principles/laws, logically connecting all content.
