In the book *How to Take Smart Notes*, another book, *The 5 Elements of Effective Thinking*, is referenced, which touches on the understanding of efficient thinking.

# Feedback Loops

A growth mindset means constantly seeking feedback. Whether you succeed or fail, adjust your understanding and habits based on that feedback. The higher the feedback frequency, the better — for example, using writing to continuously test whether you truly understand what you're learning. This is what micro-feedback means.

# Focus on the Key Points Behind the Details

You must clearly distinguish between details and key points in an article. This ability requires deliberate practice: extract the main points and express them in your own words. Improving this skill demands daily practice.

# Make Sure You See What Is Really There, Not Just What You Think Is There

The brain processes ambiguous information to make it seem coherent, which leads to illusions. Solution: when necessary, double-check and ask yourself, "Does what I understand match what the content actually says?"

# Pay Attention to What You Haven't Thought Of (or Seen) but Is Relevant to the Problem

Example: survivorship bias. Learn from others' failures — understand what doesn't work (for companies, track failure cases), not just success stories. In this example, successful cases exist within the overall picture we see, while failures do not. If we only study success stories, we might mistake "charisma/persistence" as the key factor for success.

Solutions:

1. Ask yourself: "What if...?" We tend to ignore problems that already have solutions and only see the immediate problem. Example: to understand the role of money in society, ask yourself how people would exchange goods without it. Principle: rediscovering solutions to already-solved problems is far more important than only focusing on the immediate problem at hand.

2. Take simple ideas seriously. Simple ideas are often overlooked, yet they matter greatly. Example: everyone knows the idea of buying stocks — "buy low, sell high" — but having the idea doesn't mean you understand it. When you realize that buying a share means becoming part-owner of a company, your understanding deepens. Simple does not equal easy. You should never complicate something that is simple. Example: a stock represents a share of a company; its price is determined by the market, i.e., supply and demand, which involves the rationality and valuation of market participants, which means you must understand the business you're investing in — including competition, competitive advantages, technological developments, etc. Simple ideas can form coherent and complex theories (e.g., calculus of variations).
