# Reaching Out to Professors: The Research Prep Is the Unlock

**One sentence**: The hard part of approaching a professor is not getting access — it is having something to say; reading their research first converts you from a stranger asking for time into someone who has actually engaged with their work.

## The Barrier Is Not Access

Professors are reachable. Their emails are public, their offices have hours, their labs run seminars. What stops most students is not that the door is locked — it is that there is nothing to say on the other side of it.

"I'd love to learn from you" gives them nothing to respond to. It is a request for their time with no content attached, and it reads the same whether you know their field or have never heard of it. The message isn't rejected because the sender is unknown; it's rejected because it's empty.

The prep is what fills it. Once you've read two or three of their papers, you have a specific question, and the question itself is the credential — it proves you did work nobody asked you to do.

## Source Triage by Freshness

Three places hold information about the same professor, and they run on different clocks:

| Source | What it's actually good for | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| University faculty page | Who they are, their stable bio, the area they've long worked in | Stale — often lags or omits recent work entirely |
| Google Scholar | The current edge — latest papers, the thrust of present work | Bare papers; no context on the person or group |
| Lab WeChat official account (公众号) | The lab's live pulse — recruitment, seminars, student projects, news | Nobody thinks to check it |

The official page answers *who they are*; Scholar answers *what they're working on now*. These diverge more than you'd expect — a faculty page can be years behind a professor's actual research.

The WeChat account (公众号) is the underrated one. A lab's public account carries what neither academic source does: who's joining, what talks are coming, what the students are building. It is the most current channel of the three and the one almost nobody checks — which is exactly what makes it useful. Arriving with something you read there signals attention to the living group, not just the publication record.

**The general principle**: for any person or organization you're about to approach, the freshest signal usually lives in the informal channel, not the official one.

## AI as the Reading-Level Bridge

Papers are written for peers. An undergraduate hitting a paper cold is blocked by the reading level long before the ideas — and a blocked reader gives up, which is why "just read the papers" is advice that doesn't survive contact with a real PDF.

Having an AI explain the paper collapses that barrier. The goal of the pass is not the math; it's three things:

1. **The problem** — what gap is this paper claiming to fill?
2. **The approach** — what did they do about it?
3. **The open questions** — what does it leave unresolved?

Those three are what a conversation can actually be built on. The derivations are not.

**The honest limit**: having a paper explained is not the same as understanding the field. It buys a legitimate entry point and a real question — not expertise. The failure mode is overclaiming, arriving as though you understand the subfield when you've read three summaries. The prep works precisely because it's modest: you're not pretending to be a peer, you're showing up as someone who cared enough to read.

## Why the Prep Compounds

Reading one professor's work prepares one conversation. Reading five in the same subfield does something different — the shared problem space comes into view, the questions the whole area is circling, the disagreements between groups.

At that point you're no longer asking about a paper. You're asking about the field. That is a different conversation, with a different kind of person willing to have it.
