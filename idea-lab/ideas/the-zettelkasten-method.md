> A simple note-organizing tool and its accompanying method for improving thinking and writing ability. Its goal is to provide an external knowledge base that facilitates thought.

# Tool Requirements
- An electronic card box, such as *Obsidian*
- A portable notebook for recording "fleeting notes"
- 4 note-processing zones: fleeting notes, literature notes, permanent notes, project notes

- **Fleeting notes**: Carry with you at all times. Record ideas and inspirations as they arise. Write down thoughts in complete sentences — they serve as raw material for permanent notes.
- **Literature notes**: When reading, for content worth recording or potentially useful for future thinking or writing, express it in your own words after understanding it. Keep it concise, but use complete sentences. After finishing the note, write the source title (book name) and chapter (page number) on the other side.
- **Permanent notes**: Each day, review fleeting notes and the latest literature notes, and pull out previously written permanent notes. Think about how the first two connect to the permanent notes (e.g., conflicting viewpoints, complementary viewpoints, or how a term from one context expresses itself in another). The goal is to generate derivative ideas, viewpoints, or discussions through the collision of ideas. Permanent notes should be carefully crafted and written in the most formal style.
- **Project notes**: Only relevant to a specific project. Created when a project begins, archived or discarded when it ends. Includes (project-related memos, drafts, to-do items, outlines, etc.)

# Execution Method
1. Record fleeting notes at any time
2. Record literature notes while reading, following the requirements above
3. Each day, review the latest fleeting notes and literature notes. Create new permanent notes by comparing them against past permanent notes. Afterwards, discard the corresponding fleeting notes and archive the literature notes.
4. For each new permanent note, check which previous permanent notes it relates to. Place it after those notes via links. If it relates to none, place it after the last note, and name it sequentially. (If it is closely related to a particular note, it can be placed right after it.)
5. Based on the note content, look for material in the areas where note chains and ideas are richest, and raise questions or ideas.
6. Confirm the writing topic, arrange the relevant notes in order, and fill in any gaps.
7. Write the first draft, transforming note content into coherent prose.
8. Edit and proofread the draft into a finished piece.

# Principles for Improving Thinking and Writing
## Why Writing Is Necessary
Learning is independent research — there is no clear method or goal. The main purpose is to acquire unforeseeable insights and approaches, which only have value when shared with others, hence writing is needed as the vehicle.

## Wrong Writing vs. Right Writing
Traditional writing is linear: start by determining the topic, then execute step by step, searching for relevant materials, etc. But a person cannot predict sudden ideas and insights, and therefore cannot adjust direction in time. There is a term called the "hermeneutic circle": based on one's existing knowledge framework, read extensively around multiple topics, find good questions and new writing angles, and then gain a new understanding of the knowledge framework. Writing ascends in cycles, and card-based notes are guided by interest — a process of gradually supplementing and refining what has been learned.

## Principles
The more permanent notes there are, the less they remain isolated facts and the more they become a complex system that includes mature ideas. At the same time, it becomes easier to encounter related notes, forming connections and generating derivative ideas. More is better. This is known as the "compound effect."

## Why Can It Be Sustained?
In writing, expressing book content in your own words is actually a micro-feedback on whether the knowledge has been firmly grasped — it tests whether you truly understood the content. The more you do this, the more your writing ability and thinking ability improve, and thus you do it faster — this is a "virtuous feedback loop." The process of turning fleeting notes and literature notes into permanent notes works the same way.

Because the "feedback" is dense in time and small in magnitude, successes are frequent and failures are acceptable. This makes it possible to persist without depleting willpower.

# Supplement
1. You can create an index, linking important notes to it, serving as an "entry point."
2. Admittedly, we cannot (nor need we) summarize the entire card box, but we can provide an overview of a specific topic at the note level. (This means using one note to outline the structure of a topic, and this note can be revised in the future.)
3. **Keyword** selection: Ask yourself one question: **If I have already forgotten it, under what circumstances would I stumble upon this note?** If this note contributes to a particular question (especially one you are interested in), use that question as the keyword.
4. Two types of links: **index** links that connect to **topic overviews** (which are also notes), where you can find links to the topic or related questions with brief explanatory text, and **regular links between ordinary notes**.
5. Connections between new notes and old notes:
   - Differences within similar ideas
   - Discovering contradictions, paradoxes, or oppositions
