# Distance Is Information — Why Heterogeneous Exposure Breaks Thinking Boundaries

**One sentence**: The value of an encounter scales with its *distance* from what you already are — near people and near fields are redundant, far ones carry non-redundant information, and that non-redundancy is what breaks thinking boundaries.

## The Observation

Coffee chats brought contact with people from entirely different majors, whose ways of thinking were nothing like the existing ones. Forums brought the vocabulary and live questions of fields never touched before. The felt effect was of thinking boundaries being broken, repeatedly.

(Worth stating plainly: "boundaries broken" is a feeling, not a measurement. The mechanism below is the part that stands on its own — the felt effect is not evidence for it.)

## Why Distance, Not Quantity

The information a contact carries is roughly their **distance from your existing network**.

Ten people who think the way you do tell you one thing. One person who doesn't tells you something you could not have generated yourself — not because they're smarter, but because they occupy a region of the space you don't.

The consequence is that **counting contacts is the wrong metric**. A large network of similar people is redundant; a small network of distant ones is informative. What matters is not how many nodes are attached but how far away the ones you're attached to are.

## Three Things Distance Does

1. **Exposes invisible defaults.** Your assumptions are invisible from the inside — you cannot see the water you swim in. Someone operating on different defaults makes yours visible by contrast, without either of you having to argue about it.

2. **Supplies alternative vocabularies.** Different fields have different words for overlapping phenomena. A new vocabulary is a **re-description tool**: it lets you reframe your own problem in terms that were not previously available to you. This is often the whole benefit — the model didn't change, the language did.

3. **Routes opportunities that don't travel through your own network.** The business competition arrived through a coffee-chat contact, not through any existing channel. Opportunities move through distant ties precisely because close ones are redundant: everyone near you already knows what you know, so nothing new can arrive from them.

## Two Dimensions of Heterogeneity

| Dimension | Channel | What it yields |
|---|---|---|
| **Who** you know | Coffee chats | Broken defaults, alternative framings, routed opportunities |
| **What** you know | Forums, public talks | A field's vocabulary and current questions |

The second is worth separating out, because it bears on how a new field gets entered. The usual answer to "how do I bootstrap a core in a field I know nothing about" is to reason from first principles and accept that the starting core is a guess. But a public talk supplies a **better starting core for free** — the field's actual vocabulary and its live questions, which is closer to what its practitioners consider the shape of the problem than anything derivable from outside.

So the bootstrap core doesn't have to be invented. A lecture, taken down and organized, is already a candidate.

## Why Act on What It Surfaces

Heterogeneity generates opportunities, and opportunities arriving from distant contacts share a characteristic payoff shape: **capped downside, open upside.**

The competition illustrates it exactly. Failing the first round still produced real growth. Passing — unlikely — would have produced substantially more. The loss branch is bounded and cheap; the gain branch is unbounded and improbable.

When the downside is capped and the upside isn't, **acting is worth it regardless of the probability.** Judging such an opportunity by its success probability misreads the structure — a 10% chance of a large gain with a near-zero cost of failure is not a bad bet, and it doesn't become one by being unlikely. The low-probability branch is precisely the one that pays.

This is why the correct default response to a suggestion from a distant contact is usually to take it. The suggestion is itself a product of the heterogeneity — it's an opportunity you could not have generated — and the payoff shape means the cost of declining is systematically underestimated.

## Limits and Open Questions

- **Distance has a cost.** Distant contacts share less context, require more translation, and are harder to sustain. There is presumably an **optimum rather than a maximum** — past some point, the communication overhead eats the information gain.
- **Exposure is partly involuntary.** A university mixes people by default; most later settings don't. Whether the effect can be manufactured deliberately, at the same rate, is untested.
- **Does heterogeneity compound?** The competition came via one contact, who came via a coffee chat. If distant people introduce you to further distant people, the process is self-amplifying — but that's an inference from a single chain, not an observation.
- **Is the "capped downside" always true?** Competition entries have bounded costs. Time commitments, reputational stakes, and irreversible decisions are not capped, and the reasoning above applies only where the loss branch genuinely terminates.
