Let us explore a question: where does innovation come from?

When we talk about innovation, we tend to picture a scene: someone suddenly has a flash of insight, a disruptive idea strikes out of nowhere — this seems to be where innovation lives.

# Question 1: Who is this person? What traits do they have?

A novice in a field will never produce cutting-edge innovation. On one hand, they are still in the learning phase — unable to grasp the big picture of the field, struggling to connect the knowledge in their mind. (For example, when I was studying in the PKU Gifted Program, I only understood algebra at the concept level — I had barely seen a few applications, let alone grasped its essence.) On the other hand, as a novice, even if they produce a new idea, it has almost certainly been proposed by someone before them.

The opposite of a novice is a veteran, or an expert. Corresponding to the two points above, the expert has already developed an intuitive feel for the field, and their concrete operations have become second nature through practice. Formal derivations no longer drain their energy, allowing them to focus entirely on the essence of the problem. And experts, being at the frontier of their field, become the primary originators of innovation.

# Question 2: What are the prerequisites for innovation?

From the answer to Question 1, two elements stand out: 1. Intuition, 2. Proficiency.

The relationship between them is straightforward: proficiency leads to intuition.

A person practices the knowledge of their field, becomes proficient in operations, encounters many situations, and learns how to respond under different conditions. Over time, this becomes intuition.

# Question 3: What ingrained belief does Question 2 overturn?

"We should form the habit of thinking about what we are doing."

Many famous figures say this, but it completely contradicts reality. For an ordinary person, energy is a finite resource — it depletes quickly and recovers slowly. Enforcing this kind of reflection wastes precious energy on retrospection.

The correct formulation is: "We should increase the number of things we can do without thinking."

Experts are experts precisely because they have turned theory (such as formulas) into intuition. Understanding complex situations costs them little energy — it has become something they do without thinking, which is why they have surplus energy for innovation.

The chain now looks like this: beginner → learning knowledge → deliberately applying knowledge in practice to understand it → integrated understanding → knowledge internalized as habit, producing intuition → ... (the above process loops) → becoming an expert → innovation.

This closely resembles the "hermeneutic cycle" (understanding the field through existing knowledge → reading extensively around a theme → finding good questions and new writing angles → a new understanding of the knowledge system). In essence, this is also a process of internalizing deductive steps into intuition through a practice called "thinking," thereby conserving energy and redirecting it toward more complex thought.

# Question 4: What methods promote innovation?

We have already established that "energy" is a critical resource. How you deploy energy — spending it on derivation versus focusing on essence — determines whether you are a novice or a master, and whether innovation is possible.

Therefore, improving the utilization rate of energy is a fundamental approach. Translated into action:

1. **Foster creativity through constraints**: When dealing with source material, take notes in a standardized way (for instance, all notes follow the same format). The benefit is reducing energy spent on formatting, allowing full focus on the content of the note.
2. **Interest-guided work**: Whenever possible, do things that interest you (doing uninteresting things consumes willpower, which is also a finite resource). Ask yourself: what is interesting about this task? Of course, interest is not static — by elaborating on problems encountered during work, we can also discover new interests.

A conceptual distinction: constraints (not letting people decide the format themselves) seem to restrict behavioral freedom, while interest-guided work demands autonomous choice — it requires freedom. Yet both simultaneously benefit innovation. Why?

The reason is that the nature of the two things differs. Freedom of format is a distraction, whereas freedom of interest is an enabler. Think of a person as an engine: the friction is the energy lost in deciding formats, and the fuel is interest. Both reducing friction and adding fuel benefit innovation.

# Question 5: If cultivating the ability to do something without thinking is so important in the "innovation chain," why is habit formation so difficult in daily life, even when we urgently want to build new habits? What can be done to improve this?

This is because ideas (or rational goals) have very little force. To see what a person is up to in the short term, look at their goals (e.g., wanting to lose weight, they go to the gym). But to see their long-term behavior, just look at what they have been doing for the past year or two — it does not change.

The improvement method: strategically and gradually introduce new habits, step by step replacing old ones. The new habit must be simple enough (or at least appear to be).

# Summary

This article has laid out the "innovation chain," analyzed the roles of 1. energy and 2. automatic habits, and offered suggestions from both angles on how to become an expert and achieve innovation.
