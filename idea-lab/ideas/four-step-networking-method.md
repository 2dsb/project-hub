# The Networking Method: Compliment, Introduce, Connect, Ask, Follow Up

## Source

Synthesized from real practice at a CKGSB (Cheung Kong Graduate School of Business) event, where the author successfully connected with professors, entrepreneurs, and senior professionals. Stress-tested on the same day.

## The Method

Four steps, in order:

### Step 1: Genuine Compliment + Connection Request

Open with sincere appreciation tailored to the person's role, then explicitly ask to connect.

- **If they spoke on stage (professor/speaker)**: "Your talk was incredibly insightful — I learned a lot. May I introduce myself?"
- **If they're an entrepreneur**: Express genuine admiration for their work. "I've been following what you're building — it's impressive. Could I get to know you?"

Key: the compliment must be **specific and genuine**. Generic flattery is transparent. Specific praise signals that you actually paid attention.

**Refinement — make it answerable.** "Your talk was insightful" is a conversational dead end; they can only say "thanks" and the exchange stalls. Instead, connect the compliment to something they can respond to: "Your point about X made me rethink Y — I'd love to hear more when you have time." Now they have a thread to pull on, and the conversation becomes a dialogue rather than a one-sided praise.

### Step 2: Self-Introduction (Identity Anchoring)

State who you are, concisely. Give them a clear identity anchor to remember you by.

- Today's version: "My father brought me here to learn. I just finished the Gaokao and will be studying at Peking University, School of Mathematical Sciences."
- The formula: **Who brought you / why you're here + your current stage + what's next**

The goal is not to impress — it's to give them a mental hook. "The PKU math freshman" is a clean, memorable label.

**Refinement — "my father brought me" has a shelf life.** At 18, it works: it's honest, endearing, and signals humility. By 20, the frame should shift to your own agency: "I'm researching X and wanted to learn from people in the field." The formula evolves from *who brought you* → *what you're pursuing and why you chose to be here*.

### Step 3: Contact Exchange + Learning Intent

Ask for their contact information, and frame the ask around learning from them:

- "Could I add you on WeChat?"
- "I'd be honored to learn from you going forward."
- "It would be a privilege to receive your guidance."

This does two things: (a) secures the connection, and (b) positions you as someone who respects their expertise — which makes them more willing to stay in touch.

### Step 4: Respectful, Specific Question

End with a question that shows genuine curiosity about their work:

- "What industry are you in?"
- "What are you working on right now?"
- "What's the hardest problem you're currently tackling?"

The question serves multiple purposes: it keeps the conversation going, it gives you information, and it demonstrates that you're not just collecting contacts — you're actually interested.

**Refinement — don't ask what you should already know.** "What industry are you in?" is too basic if they just spoke on stage or their company is publicly known. It signals you didn't do your homework. Instead, ask something that shows you paid attention but want to go deeper: "I saw you're in autonomous driving — what's the hardest part of scaling that right now?" Specificity is respect. The question should sit in the sweet spot between *what's publicly available* and *what only someone on the inside would know*.

### Step 5: Follow-Up Within 24 Hours (The Step That Makes the Other Four Count)

The method doesn't end at the handshake. A WeChat contact is not a relationship — it's a door. The follow-up is what walks through it.

- **Timing**: Within 24 hours, while their memory of you is still warm. Beyond 48 hours, you become "that person from the event" — a vague face, not a specific individual.
- **Message structure**: Reference something specific from your conversation. Not "Nice to meet you" — that's generic. Instead: "Really appreciated your point about X at the event yesterday. It made me think about Y. Looking forward to learning more from you." This proves you were listening and are still thinking about it.
- **Don't ask for anything yet**: The first follow-up is purely about reinforcing the connection. Asking for a favor or advice in the same message turns the interaction transactional. Let the relationship breathe first.
- **Long game**: The goal is not a one-time exchange. It's becoming someone they remember and think of when opportunities arise. That requires periodic, low-pressure touchpoints — sharing an article they'd find interesting, congratulating them on a milestone, or updating them on something you discussed.

## Why This Works

1. **Order matters**: Compliment first lowers defenses; introduction gives them a reason to care; contact ask is natural once rapport exists; question extends the conversation beyond the exchange; follow-up converts a contact into a relationship
2. **Low pressure, high respect**: Every step signals deference without groveling. "I want to learn from you" is flattering but not needy
3. **Memorable anchoring**: "PKU math freshman" + specific compliment = they'll remember you when you follow up
4. **Scalable**: The same 5-step structure works on professors, founders, executives, and peers — only the content of each step adapts
5. **The follow-up is the multiplier**: Steps 1-4 without step 5 = a good first impression that fades. Steps 1-4 + step 5 = a relationship that compounds
