Welfare Economics: the study of how resource allocation affects economic well-being.

# Consumer Surplus

**Willingness to Pay**: the maximum amount a buyer is willing to pay for a good (reflects the buyer's valuation of the good).

**Consumer Surplus**: the amount a buyer is willing to pay minus the amount they actually pay.

## Relationship to the Demand Curve

- The price given by the demand curve represents the marginal buyer's willingness to pay.
- Consumer surplus equals the area below the demand curve and above the price line.

# Producer Surplus

**Cost**: the value of everything a seller must give up to produce a good (direct expenditures, time, etc.).

**Producer Surplus**: the amount a seller receives minus their cost.

## Relationship to the Supply Curve

- The price given by the supply curve represents the marginal seller's cost.
- Producer surplus equals the area above the supply curve and below the price line.

# Market Efficiency

Total Surplus = Consumer Surplus + Producer Surplus = Buyer's Valuation − Seller's Cost

A resource allocation is called **efficient** if it maximizes total surplus.

**Efficiency**: the property of a resource allocation that maximizes total surplus for all members of society.

**Equality**: the property of distributing economic outcomes evenly among members of society.

Three observations about market outcomes:

1. Free markets allocate the supply of goods to the buyers who value them most.
2. Free markets allocate the demand for goods to the sellers who can produce at the lowest cost.
3. Free markets produce the quantity of goods that maximizes total surplus.

# Assumptions Underlying Market Efficiency

1. **Market Power**: the market is perfectly competitive — everyone is a price taker, no market power exists.
2. **Externalities**: the decisions of buyers and sellers do not affect the well-being of bystanders.

These two conditions are collectively referred to as **market failure**.
