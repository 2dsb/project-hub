This refers to the process from the emergence of a new technology to its replacement by an even newer technology (here, "technology" is used in the plural sense).

## 1. Origin

Driven by demand, a fundamentally new technology makes its debut with its new principles. At this stage, the technology is still highly immature — its components are sometimes cobbled together awkwardly, and its capabilities are unremarkable.

## 2. Development

The initial new technology takes a long time to emerge, but once it appears, a technology domain rapidly forms around it. Specifically, derivative technologies spring up like bamboo shoots after rain.

The creators of derivative technologies include the original technology's inventors, later entrants to the field, laboratories, and enterprises. Derivative technologies arise because:
- Different actors define and conceptualize the technology from different perspectives
- They pursue different purposes
If the original technology has commercial or military applications, derivative technologies emerge at an even faster rate.

From the perspective of external application, the technology also has internal drivers for development:
- Strengthening basic performance
- Monitoring environmental changes and anomalies to enhance responsiveness
- Adapting to a broader range of tasks
- Enhancing safety and reliability

## 3. Setbacks

Setbacks refer to the limitations encountered during a technology's evolution. Sources of limitations:
- They can come from the technology itself, specifically the limits of the phenomena being exploited
- They can come from the limits of external functionality

Two methods are used to address limitations:
- **Internal replacement**: replacing the limiting component (sub-technology) with a better one. The better component may originate from outside the technology domain, so internal replacement is tied to the development of external technologies.
- **Structural deepening**: retaining the original limiting component and supplementing it with sub-layers to strengthen its function and assist it in completing its work, in order to break through the limitation.

## 4. Maturity

The maturity of a technology (or technology domain) means that its performance can no longer be improved even through internal replacement and structural deepening. At this point, the technology is refined and complex.

However, the demand to "further improve performance" persists, and newer technologies (based on principles different from the original) emerge. Yet the old technology is still far from obsolescence. This lag phenomenon is called **lock-in**:
- From the perspective of reality vs. potential: the old technology is refined while the new one is crude; the former's current capabilities exceed the latter's, while the latter's potential has not yet been fully developed.
- From the perspective of marginal cost: transitioning from old to new technology requires changing surrounding structures and organizations, with high economic and other costs.
- From the psychological perspective: practitioners do not buy into the vision and promises of the new principles; the new technology renders the old one obsolete, creating cognitive dissonance between the unknown new principles and the safe old ones, leading to an emotional mismatch.

The phenomenon caused by technological lock-in is called **adaptive extension**: when faced with demands that (may) exceed the limits of a mature technology, people tend to solve problems and meet demands using the old principles and old technology.

## 5. Replacement

After a technology matures, more and more new demands can no longer be satisfied through adaptive extension. New principles thus gain a foothold to move forward, restarting [The Cycle of Technology](../permanent-notes/33-技术的循环.md), gradually developing and becoming refined.

# Purposive System Layers

- For non-technological purposive systems (such as military systems), structural deepening leads to encrustation — gradual bloating, with outdated institutions (analogous to components) becoming difficult to change.
- Scientific theoretical systems also follow a similar cycle (a periodic structure).

After a theory emerges, driven by the facts it confronts and the applications it is forced into, its constituent elements are replaced; when it encounters limitations (anomalies), the theory deepens; when faced with exceptions that do not fit the theory, the theory extends; until enough variations accumulate that the theory's efficacy declines and a new theory is needed.
