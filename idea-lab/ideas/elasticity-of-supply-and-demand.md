# Demand Elasticity

**Price elasticity of demand**: measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price.
Elastic vs. inelastic.

Determinants:
1. Availability of close substitutes
     Goods with close substitutes tend to be more elastic.
2. Necessities vs. luxuries
     Necessities are inelastic; luxuries are elastic.
3. Market definition
     Elasticity in a narrowly defined market > elasticity in a broadly defined market (ice cream market elasticity > food market elasticity).
     Goods in narrowly defined markets have substitutes.

Calculating price elasticity of demand:
Price elasticity of demand = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price (typically considered in absolute value).
*The steeper the demand curve, the smaller the elasticity; the flatter the demand curve, the larger the elasticity.*
Midpoint method: for two points A and B on the demand curve, to calculate the price elasticity of demand from A to B, use the midpoint as the base for percentages.
For example,
$A(Q_1,P_1),B(Q_2,P_2),elasticity=\frac{(Q_1-Q_2)/[(Q_1+Q_2)/2]}{(P_1-P_2)/[(P_1+P_2)/2]}$

Elasticity > 1: elastic
Elasticity < 1: inelastic
Elasticity = 1: unit elastic

Total revenue: the amount paid by buyers and received by sellers of a good, calculated as price times quantity sold.
- When demand is inelastic, price and total revenue move in the same direction;
- When demand is elastic, price and total revenue move in opposite directions;
- When demand is unit elastic, price changes do not affect total revenue.

**Income elasticity of demand** = % change in quantity demanded / % change in income
**Cross-price elasticity of demand** = % change in quantity demanded of good 1 / % change in price of good 2

# Supply Elasticity

**Price elasticity of supply**: measures how responsive quantity supplied is to a change in price.
Elastic vs. inelastic.

Determinants:
1. Flexibility of sellers to change the quantity of the good they produce
     Beachfront land is inelastic because you cannot produce more of it;
     Books, cars, and TVs are elastic because when prices rise, factories can run longer hours.
2. Time horizon: long-run elasticity exceeds short-run elasticity
     In the short run, firms cannot easily reduce or increase production of a given good, but in the long run, firms can build new factories, making their long-run responsiveness to price greater than in the short run.

Calculating price elasticity of supply:
Price elasticity of supply = % change in quantity supplied / % change in price (typically considered in absolute value).
*The steeper the supply curve, the smaller the elasticity; the flatter the supply curve, the larger the elasticity.*
Midpoint method: for two points A and B on the supply curve, to calculate the price elasticity of supply from A to B, use the midpoint as the base for percentages.
For example,
$A(Q_1,P_1),B(Q_2,P_2),elasticity=\frac{(Q_1-Q_2)/[(Q_1+Q_2)/2]}{(P_1-P_2)/[(P_1+P_2)/2]}$

Elasticity > 1: *elastic*
Elasticity < 1: inelastic
Elasticity = 1: unit elastic

A firm's production capacity has a maximum. When quantity supplied is low, supply elasticity is very high; when quantity supplied is high, supply elasticity is very low.

# Applications

## Can Good News for Farming Be Bad News for Farmers?

You are a wheat farmer. To earn more money, you work hard to increase your land's productivity (the quantity of goods or services produced per unit of labor input).

One day, Kansas State University develops a new hybrid wheat variety that increases wheat yield by 20%. Do farmers now earn more or less?

Question 1: Which curve shifts?
    The demand curve stays the same; the supply curve shifts right.
Question 2: How do equilibrium price and quantity change?
    Equilibrium price falls; equilibrium quantity increases.
Question 3: Do farmers gain or lose?
    Total revenue for wheat = equilibrium price × equilibrium quantity (P × Q). But wheat is price-inelastic — price drops significantly (in percentage terms), while quantity increases only slightly. This causes revenue to fall. Farmers lose.
Question 4: If farmers lose, why do they still adopt the new variety?
    This gets at the heart of how competitive markets work. Each farmer is a price taker who treats the price as given. At any given price of wheat, a farmer using the new variety produces and sells more wheat, increasing their income. But when everyone does this, wheat supply increases, the price falls, and farmers end up worse off.

## Why Can't OPEC Maintain High Oil Prices?

In the short run, both oil supply and demand are inelastic.
- Supply is inelastic: known oil reserves and extraction capacity cannot change rapidly.
- Demand is inelastic: consumption habits do not immediately respond to price changes.
In the short run, a small reduction in oil supply leads to a large price increase.

In the long run:
- Supply: non-OPEC oil producers respond to high prices by intensifying oil exploration and building new extraction capacity.
- Demand: consumers become more frugal, replacing old gas-guzzling cars with new fuel-efficient models.
This makes both the long-run supply curve and demand curve more elastic.
Therefore, in the long run, a small reduction in oil supply leads to a much smaller price change.

The conclusion: raising oil prices is easier in the short run than in the long run.
