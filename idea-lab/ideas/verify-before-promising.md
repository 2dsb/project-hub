# Verify Before Promising — A Commitment Is a Bound, Not a Feasibility Claim

**One sentence**: A promise is a claim about a **bound** ("I can finish this within a budget"), not about feasibility ("I can do this") — and because a strong commitment ethic means you'll pay any cost rather than break it, the check has to happen *before* the promise, since your conscience removes the failure signal that would otherwise teach you.

## The Event

After a lecture, the commitment was to organize the content and send it that day. Keeping commitments is something that matters a great deal here — so when the AI image work blew past its estimate, the response was not to send it late or send it partial, but to keep going. The work finished at 2am.

Nothing was broken. The promise was kept. That's exactly what makes it a useful case: **the failure is invisible.**

## The Wrong Question

The check that didn't happen is usually stated as: *"can I get this done?"*

That question is nearly useless, because the answer is almost always yes. You can get almost anything done — if you're willing to stay up until 2am. Feasibility is not the binding constraint; **the budget is.** The promise wasn't "I can do this," it was "I can do this *today, within a normal working day, without wrecking tomorrow*." That's a claim about a bound, and bounds are what need checking.

So the pre-commitment question is not *can I?* but:

> **Can I bound this? Do I know what it would take, and is there a ceiling on how long it could run?**

If there's no ceiling, there's nothing to promise — because you don't know what you're committing to.

## Why the Wrong Check Feels Right

For most tasks, feasibility and boundedness give the same answer. You know how long things take, so "can I do it?" silently *includes* "within budget." The check passes without ever being examined.

The gap opens only when the estimate is unreliable — which is precisely when the check matters most. So the naive check degrades exactly in the situation it exists for: it keeps returning a confident "yes" while saying nothing about the bound. (The AI image task was the case that exposed this — see the related idea on what makes AI task cost unbounded.)

## The Ethic Is an Amplifier, Not a Guardrail

It's tempting to treat conscientiousness as a safety net: *I care about my commitments, so I won't overpromise.* The opposite is closer to true.

A strong commitment ethic guarantees the cost gets paid. It doesn't prevent the bad estimate — it determines **who absorbs it**. Someone who would let a promise slip converts an estimation error into a broken promise; someone who won't, converts it into a late night. Same error, different distribution.

Worse, the ethic **destroys the feedback loop.** Broken promises are loud, socially costly, and memorable — they teach. Late nights are quiet, private, and read as diligence rather than failure. So the estimate never registers as having been wrong, and the correction never fires. The better your commitment ethic, the *less* signal you get about your own estimation errors.

## What to Do Instead

1. **Check the bound, not the feasibility.** Before promising, answer: what would this take, and what's the worst case? If you can't answer, you don't have a promise yet — you have a hope.
2. **If you can't bound it, promise something else.** The choice isn't binary between overpromising and saying nothing. You can promise a *later* date (buying variance), promise the *attempt* without a deadline ("I'll give it a go and send whatever I have"), or promise a bounded subset ("I'll send the text portion today").
3. **Bound the task before accepting it.** A timebox plus a fallback deliverable makes an unbounded task promiseable — because now the ceiling is defined by you, not by the task.
4. **Treat a late night as a signal, not a badge.** The failure to notice is the expensive part. If a commitment cost far more than expected, that's an estimate error to log — the same information a broken promise would have delivered.

## Open Questions

- **Where's the line between diligence and a design flaw?** Absorbing the tail looks virtuous and is locally good for the person you promised to. But it subsidizes bad estimating, which will eventually cost someone more than a late delivery would have.
- **Should commitments be priced with an explicit confidence?** "I'll have it today, ~80%" is more honest than a flat promise — but most social contexts read hedging as a lack of commitment, so the honest form may be socially penalized.
- **Does this generalize to self-promises?** A commitment to yourself has no external party to disappoint, which removes the last check on overcommitting — and arguably makes the bound check even more necessary.
