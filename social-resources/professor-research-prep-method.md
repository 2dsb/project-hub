# Professor Research Prep

## The Idea

A method for getting to the point where you can actually talk to a professor. The bottleneck is almost never access — it's having nothing to say. This playbook solves that by preparing from their research before you reach out, so you arrive with a real question instead of a request for time.

The method was learned from someone who does this in practice: ask an AI which professors at your target university work on your field, pull those professors' papers, have an AI explain them, then approach.

Works on any university with public faculty pages. Works best in person, after the prep.

## The Method

### 1. Find the professors

Ask an AI: *"Which professors at [university] research [your field of interest]?"* This gets you a shortlist fast. Verify against the university's own department pages before you commit — AI-generated lists can miss people, drift on spelling, or include faculty who've moved.

### 2. Pull their papers — from the right source for the right question

Three sources, three different time horizons. Use them for what each is actually good for:

| Source | What to get from it |
|---|---|
| **University official page** | Who they are — the stable bio, their title, the area they've long worked in. Treat this as background, not as a picture of current work: these pages are frequently out of date and may not list their latest papers at all. |
| **Google Scholar** | What they're working on now. This is where the newest papers are. Search their name, sort by year, and read the most recent 2–3. |
| **The lab's WeChat official account (公众号)** | The lab's live pulse — who's recruiting, what seminars are running, what the students are building. Many labs run one. Neither of the above carries this. |

Don't skip the third one. It's the channel almost nobody checks, and it's where you'll find the things that make an approach feel current — an upcoming talk, a project the group just announced, a recruiting notice.

### 3. Have an AI explain the papers

Reading the paper cold is the step where most people quit — papers are written for peers, and the math blocks you long before the ideas do. Paste it in and ask for three things:

- **What problem is this solving?** The gap they claim to fill.
- **What's the approach?** What they actually did about it.
- **What's left open?** What the paper doesn't resolve.

Skip the derivations. You're not trying to reproduce the work — you're trying to be able to hold a conversation about it. Two or three papers is plenty; you're looking for the shape of their research, not a literature review.

### 4. Turn the reading into the approach

Now you have something to say. Reach out with a specific question grounded in what you read — ideally one that sits between what's publicly available and what only someone on the inside would know.

Not: *"I'm interested in your research and would love to learn from you."*
But: *"I read your recent paper on X — I was struck by how you handled Y. Does that approach hold when Z?"*

The second one can only be written by someone who read the paper. That's the whole point.

## Why It Works

- **It solves the actual bottleneck.** The obstacle isn't that professors are unreachable — it's that an empty message gives them nothing to respond to. Prep fills the message.
- **The question is the credential.** A specific question proves you did unpaid work nobody asked for. That's a stronger signal than any self-description.
- **The source split catches what a single source misses.** Official pages go stale, Scholar shows the current edge, the lab's public account shows the living group. Together they give you both the person and the moment.
- **The AI pass removes the reading-level block.** It moves you from "I can't read this" to "I can talk about this" — which is the only gap that actually stands between you and the approach.
- **Know its limit.** Explained papers are not expertise. Go in modest — as someone who read, not someone who knows. That framing is both honest and more effective.

## When to Use

- Before cold-approaching a professor, researcher, or lab — by email, at a talk, or at a conference.
- Before a seminar or lab meeting you plan to attend, so you have something to contribute afterward.
- When you're choosing between several professors in a field and need to find out whose work you actually find interesting.
- Not needed when you already have an introduction, or when the person already knows you.
